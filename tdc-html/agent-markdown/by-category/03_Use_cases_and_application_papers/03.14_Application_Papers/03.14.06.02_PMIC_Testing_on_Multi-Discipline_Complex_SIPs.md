# PMIC Testing on Multi-Discipline Complex SIPs

Don Blair, Verigy

Pascale Cowie, Verigy

Paper ID: 104672

## Abstract

Power Management ICs (PMICs) have been and will continue to be in high demand because of the increasing complexity of voltages and currents required in an end user application and the complexity of the lithium ion (Li-ion) battery which is a common choice for portable consumer devices. In fact, unlike NiMH or NiCad batteries where undesirable "memory" effects are repaired by a complete discharge, a Li-ion battery would be damaged by doing this. It must stay in a narrow range of voltage and be charged at specified rates of current.

New consumer devices which are being pressured by cost have created the trend of integrated PMICs into a System-In-Package (SIP) single chip application. One good example of this is in the cell phone market place at the entry level. See Figure One, which shows all the functionality which is included in a single chip multi-die SIP cell phone IC.

 ![One Single Chip Cell Phone](<../../../../raw/104673.png>)

Different IC manufacturers may put different functions in different areas, but the overall architectures are similar. It can be seen in Figure One that the PMIC has two major roles, one: supply the power to the different Intellectual Property (IP) functions that require different voltages and two: provide the clocking and be a communication highway. The PMIC can monitor functions in different blocks. Also the digital part of the base-band processor communicates with the PMIC to control its functions.

The rest of the SIP is partitioned by RF and IF and the analog mixed signal part of the base band processor for one die. Then the other die has all of the digital functionality on it including the microcontroller, memory and DSP processing which does the spectral shaping of various components.

The trend is for this integration to continue. The fewer the ICs on a PC board in the cell phone, the less power consumption, space, cost and if designed well can even improve performance. In the consumer world, performance is very important, either the product meets its specifications or it does not, however low cost is very key to consumer demand. To summarize: performance is needed to have a product, but cost very well determines if it is a success or not.

The economics of this drive the SIP to be packaged with known good die (KGD) that have been tested at wafer sort and then demand a single insertion final package test.

The final test platform has to have multi-discipline Analog mixed signal, Digital, DC and RF capabilities and the economics drive this to a multi-site configuration. Additionally, a lot of test leverage can be added if the same ATE platform is used for both the wafer sort and the package test. Finally, the test time and capital equipment costs have to be watched carefully so the most cost effective solution can be employed.

## Introduction

In this paper the differences between testing a standalone PMIC and integrated SIP PMIC will be examined. Then conventional as well as new methodologies to accomplish the test will be explored and finally new testing trends will looked at.

## Differences between PMIC testing on a standalone IC versus SIP

Design-For-Testability (DFT) plays a major role in what is accessible and what isn't. Clearly all of the pins available in the standalone are not available in the SIP in an effort to keep the pin count manageable.

The questions are " Is there enough accessibility to make the PMIC testable?" and "Does the PMIC get tested individually or as a system?".

The answer to the first question has a large impact to the second question. Most IC manufacturers at this time are testing the PMIC with many individual tests. This provides IC designers and product engineers with feedback about the IC design, process and packaging but is more expensive.

The Test-Access-Mechanism (TAM) is the way that the PMIC and other IP blocks interface to the outside world and internally to each other in the final test package. The TAM for the chip changes the thinking on how to access desired functions. Instead of accessing by the IP block, the functions are accessed via the SIP as a whole.

For instance, the voltage regulator outputs may come to the package pins, but the communication interfaces do not. In such a case, the PMIC is powered up first and provides the clocking for the rest of the device, then the instructions can be sent to registers in the baseband processor to turn on the rest of the IC. For example in Figure One, the main I/O bus is shown in black routing to all the block sections.

The lack of isolation of pin access is another issue which affects the test methodology. Two examples of this are explored; in Figure Two, the PMIC voltage regulator output is connected to the base band processor on the load-board. This configuration is chosen to allow either PMIC or the VI32 to power-up the base band processor. Relays were not used because real-state on the load-board is not very available. Any tests which check the current output of that voltage regulator must account also for the current used by baseband processor. The second example is the PMIC_HOLD pin which passes the status back that the base band processor has successfully powered up and causes the PMIC to continue providing the clocking and staying powered up.

## Solution complexity increases for SIP

The device-under-test (DUT) board complexity increases with the SIP solution. More components, connection points and the need to route sensitive signals carefully to avoid noise and crosstalk increase with the complexity.

Also the test program length and complexity will increase as a result of testing the RF, baseband digital and analog and the PMIC all in one pass, one insertion.

## Team approach changes

Although many projects now employ a team approach to reduce the time-to-market (TTM), the complexity of the SIP and even the knowledge requirements for all of the different disciplines involved demand a multi-tiered team approach. Typically a team would be assigned to each major IP block, including PMIC, baseband digital and analog mixed signal and then RF. Each team has a lead who is responsible for directing the team as well as backups, revision control and occasional program merges with the other teams. Integrated releases can occur as often as weekly to gauge progress.

## ATE requirements

Cost-of-Test (COT) economics demand a single insertion multiple site production solution.

The RF capability needs to be multi-port source and measure capable of different modulation schemes and be able to support sensitive measurements such as Noise Figure and EVM. The Port Scale RF solution can source up to 6 GHz and measure to 8 GHz with all the common modulation schemes supported.

The digital capability needs to have fast enough speeds, capable of many pins for multi-site and low cost. Typically, a quad-site solution requires over 700 digital pins. Also, there needs to be links into the EDA tools to easily transfer vectors back and forth. Verigy PS400 cards meet these needs well.

The analog mixed signal capability needs to have multiple source and measure pins with differing speeds and resolutions. The Multi-band Audio Video 8 (MBAV8) is a good choice because it has 4 channels of source/measure audio at 24 bits of resolution and 4 channels of source/measure in baseband frequencies up to 100 MHz at 16 bits of resolution.

DC capabilities while deceptively simple are very important. The V93000 per-pin measurement unit (PPMU) can sink up to 75 mA per pin (40 mA PPMU + 35 mA of the active load for the PS800) and is available on every digital pin. These pins can also be ganged together for higher current needs. The PS400 digital pins can be licensed down to 1 MHz speed for an effectively "DC only" capability. Device Power Supplies (DPS) can be used for higher current requirements. The DC Scale VI32 was design specifically for PMIC requirements of high accuracy. It can supply and measure up to 25 V and the channels can also be ganged together for additional current. The DC Scale DPS32 is a good resource for main power being able to supply up to 1.5 amps per channel.

The Per-Pin Time Interval Analyzer is another time based measurement capability that is required to test these SIPs. The main uses of it are frequency, rise and fall time, pulse width on one pin and often a time interval measurement is needed between pins to measure timing.

Density of resources and the cooling/calibration of those resources are important. The Verigy PS400 cards supply 64 pins of digital, PPMU and PPTIA capability all on one card. The MBAV8 has 8 parallel source and measurement paths. The Port Scale RF has 24 ports of source and measure capability. The DC Scale VI32 and DPS32 each have 32 pins of DC capability. All of these cards are liquid cooled, maintaining a junction temperature of 32 degrees C which ensures calibration is stable (no warm up time and variation due to temp changes) and also prolongs uptime and serviceable life.

Scalability is also important for economic reasons. The scalability of the V93000 means that hardware and software investments are preserved even as newer features are developed to address new requirements.

One of the key tools in scaling resources is through software licensing. The product hardware comes with its full range of capability but this can be licensed up or down through software. Characterization is often done with higher capability resources that are not required in production. Being able to scale back in frequency, memory or features on the digital cards of the V93000 is a great COT saver. The MB AV8 also can scale back the number of pins available and frequency of the AWG. Another advantage of scalability is being able to change where a resource is with software. The PPTIA is a great example of how in one program the capability can be on one set of pins and different in another program. This greatly simplifies DUT board layout. Another example of pure hardware scaling is the new Compact Test Head (CTH) which can run programs and DUT boards from a large or small test head. This scalability also improves the TTM because it minimizes re-tooling (DUT board) and re-programming as a product moves through its life cycle from characterization to ramp to steady demand to end-of-life.

## Key PMIC tests

The Bandgap reference trim and test is a key test where the tester measures a voltage reference and then will blow a fuse to make the voltage more accurate as a standard. Most manufacturers perform this test at wafer sort but some at package test. Since all of the different voltages a PMIC provides are based on this standard, it is important to be accurate. The limit for the accuracy of this test is typically +/- 10 mV. For guard-banding and keeping yields acceptable the ATE accuracy needs to be +/- 1 mV or better. The DC Scale VI32 in the high accuracy mode is at +/- 200 uV accuracy.

 ![LDO line and load regulation](<../../../../raw/104906.png>)

The voltage regulator accuracy, line and load regulation tests are also basic key tests for the PMIC. Typically a DPS would supply the current and voltage at the Battery terminal ("VBAT") and at the Charger terminal ("VCHG"). One DPS or several ganged PPMU provide the loading of the regulators. A PPMU measures the regulator voltage output on the sense line, as shown in Figure Three. A better way would be to use the VI32 card because it has more accuracy than the PPMU, it meets the loading requirements of most voltage regulators and it provides a sense line. The VI32 would replace many DPSs and PPMUs and it still would allow 32 regulator outputs to be tested in parallel.

One of the major functions of the PMIC is to provide multiple voltages of well regulated power. Many variables occur including input voltage changes (line regulation) due to the Li-ion battery going down and providing the power at the rated current load output. Each test is done to measure the delivered voltage at the specified current load (load regulation) and the regulator is specifically designed for Low Dropout Voltage (LDO). Minimum and maximum current loads are placed at the output and then the voltage variation is measured.

For the load regulation results the following formula is used:

```
Load Regulation = (( Vout max - Vout min ) / (I load max - I load min) ) X 100.
```

Results for this test using the DPS 32 are shown in Figure Four. Three sites are plotted for this chart.

## Motivation to move from Legacy DC to new DC Scale resources

There are many reasons to migrate to the new DC Scale resources on the V93000.

First, let's examine the improved COT based reasons. Faster test execution times are one result of using the new DC Scale boards. Also the density is quite high at 32 individual pins per board being able to supply up to 1.5 A per pin. That reduces the number of legacy DPSs (GPDPS - 4 pins, MS-DPS - 8 pins) required and the number of digital pins used if the PPMU was the legacy DC that was being used in the past. These resources are also not locked into a specific slot in the card cage the way the legacy DPSs were. The new resources reside where the digital pin cards reside, so again the density capability is much higher than before.

This supports more multi-site and potential concurrent testing to be done.

Next, let's look at technology reasons. The new DC scale resources have higher accuracy and are test processor based. This will allow digital pins to communicate "natively" with the DC Scale resources. Already planned are sequencer commands which will allow DC source and measurement dynamically from inside a digital pattern with 64 MB of memory per pin. No digital pin is required to trigger a resource. Also, DPS measurements can be made in parallel to digital functional tests. The DC Scale resources also have some special features such as an on board AWG and digitizer and also statistical computation modes that will automatically provide minimum, maximum and averaged values. The DC Scale VI32 has a special high impedance mode (greater than 8 MOhm at 8 volts) useful for very low leakage current or measuring a voltage from a weak source.

The DC Scale resources are also code compatible with legacy programs including DPS equation based , DPS_TASK or SPMU_TASK based methods.

## Future Test Needs

Some PMIC technology trends that will impact test include lower voltages and lower power consumption driving needs for higher accuracy and lower noise floor on ATE.

Lowering the COT is probably the biggest driver of any changes likely to occur in the future. The lower the cost, the bigger the potential market; especially for entry level applications in emerging economies. Cell phones are also a good example of an appliance needing very little infrastructure. A cell tower in a dense urban area can be the infrastructure for thousands of phones.

System level test is where instead of being tested one IP block at a time, the SIP is tested end-to-end and preferably in "mission mode" where the device is being tested the same as how it will be used in the end application. In the cell phone this would amount to placing and answering a call and checking all of the data functions (digital functional tests). This also has the potential of lowering test time and capital cost of the ATE system.

The main questions would center around does this provide enough test coverage and does it give the IC designers enough information to fix errors and design better new circuits? There are two ways to address this.

One, a subset of devices could have more extensive testing to augment the system level test. The other approach would be to have a hybrid of tests that do both system level and some IP block "parametrics".

Concurrent testing of multiple IP blocks is possible from the ATE side. In fact, the V93000 per pin architecture, high density of resources, multiple clock domains and multi-port software make this available today and is being done to some extent. However, the limitation of the extent that this can be done is more on the IC design side. Designing IP blocks that can operate independent of each other and that have a TAMs designed such that ATE can access and test the blocks concurrently is being worked on by many manufactures.

More sites tested in parallel is a known way to drive costs down further. A COT matrix should be used to determine what the optimum number of sites to test for a given set of variables such as capital costs, test time, availability and cost of multi-site handlers and other potential incremental infrastructure factors.

Another trend is continued integration that is focused on lowering IC die costs and has the potential to lower the capital costs and test time much the same as the system level tests. The higher the integration, the less the individual IP blocks will be accessible through the higher integrated TAMs.

## Conclusion

The trend of the consumer market to demand that costs are reduced for mass market applications like a cell phone will continue into the future. COT is an important part of this. Full capability still needs to be there but ways need to be developed to reduce the cost. The IC manufacturers have found a way to do this by further integration of what used to be a chip set, down into a multi-die SIP.

This approach adds greatly to the complexity of the test solution including areas such as DFT discipline, increased DUT board complexity and requiring an engineering team approach to meet the TTR requirements for a complex IC. Even the test philosophy can potentially change at the higher integration level, with system level test being a viable alternative to the IP test block mentality.

ATE manufacturers need to lead similar trends in the test world. Verigy has contributed in this area across all of the disciplines and even the architecture of the platform. The architecture of the platform supports common hardware and software and even common test heads that scale from the 0 footprint Compact test heads all the way up to Large test heads capable of 4096 digital pins. Software licensing allows speed, memory, PPTIA availability and number of resources to be configured. While the licensing allows great flexibility, also the number of resources per card has increased including MB AV8 with 8, DC Scale DPS32 with 32, VI32 with 32, the Port Scale RF with 24 and the PS400 with 64 pins.

These solutions will continue to help drive down the COT for consumer applications like PMIC and the single chip cell phone as shown in Figure Six.
