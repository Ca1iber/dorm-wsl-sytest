# Extending the Capabilities of ATE Digitizers for Dynamic Testing of High Speed, High Resolution DACs with Ensemble Averaging

Bernd Laquai, Verigy

Paper-ID: 120444 April 2010

## Abstract

A key challenge when testing the more powerful next generation semiconductor devices is the fact that only today's ATE instruments is available for this purpose. For DACs contained in mixed signal SOC devices the challenge is even stronger since with DACs typically higher resolutions and higher speeds are more easily achieved compared to ADCs used in ATE equipment to digitize the DAC output of a DUT during test. This means that often the effective resolution of a DAC given in ENOBs (effective number of bits) can hardly be tested when the architectural number of bits of the ATE digitizer is only a few bits higher, than that of the new DAC under test.

This paper presents a methodology to overcome this limitation with a smart averaging algorithm that affects the test time and thus the test cost only minimally. The method is based on the fact, that the quantization noise, responsible for the ENOB performance, is deterministic in contrast to the random noise that normally limits the ATE performance. Therefore a significant performance improvement can be expected when appropriate statistical post-processing methods are applied such as the so-called ensemble averaging.

Key Words: DAC-Testing, Mixed-Signal Applications, Performance Parameters

## Introduction

The demand for processing an increasing amount of data in less time in conjunction with an immense cost pressure forces signal processing applications to squeeze out more and more of the available bandwidth in a signal path. Even though the information on the signal path is often represented with highly complex analog signals its processing is performed mostly digital. As a consequence, the requirements for A/D and D/A conversion are constantly growing. Testing of dynamic performance parameters of the conversion units at mission mode speed is therefore an imperative element in the test plan of respective SOC devices.

When the dynamic performance of a DAC is to be tested, two classes of performance parameters can be distinguished. On the one hand parameters like THD, SFDR and IMD are used that relate the energy of distortion signals at few discrete frequencies to the desired carrier. On the other hand parameters like SNR, SINAD and ENOB relate unwanted noise energy to the carrier. Whereas it is still comparably easy to measure the energy of distortions at discrete frequencies using an N+1 or N+2 bit digitizer (assuming an N bit resolution of the DUT), the correct measurement of noise is the key challenge. However, the noise level and with it the effective number of bits of the DAC provides a high fault coverage since it reveals the failure of single bits at mission mode speed.

When calculating the number of effective bits from a SNR measurement the assumption is that the noise seen by the digitizer is caused by the quantization error and random noise of the DAC only. In order to do such a measurement correctly, the digitizer should have a higher resolution than the DAC and no other noise source should perturb the measurement. Whereas a higher architectural resolution for the digitizer may still be found in an ATE, the requirement for a lower noise level is often difficult. In many cases, the total noise of the ATE digitizer is higher than the quantization noise produced by the DAC under test and therefore SNR, SINAD and the effective number of bits cannot be determined correctly in a straightforward way.

The quantization error signal is deterministic and therefore the noise produced is also deterministic. This is in contrast to the other noise sources in a test setup where a DAC is connected to an ATE digitizer. The practical experience shows, that the noise in such a setup is dominated by purely random noise caused by the analog processing units such as filters, range amplifiers and offset compensation circuits.

The methodology proposed in this paper relies on this key difference in randomness and determinism. It is based on the fact, that truly random noise which masks the deterministic quantization noise of interest can be removed by statistical methods such as simple averaging. Having removed the parasitic noise and provided the digitizer has an architectural resolution greater than the DAC under test, the accurate determination of parameters such as SNR, SINAD and the deterministic ENOB will be possible despite of relatively high total noise levels in the ATE system.

This approach implies that some loss of resolution is expected by a non ideal functionality of the DAC under test.

In reality however, the loss of SNR based ENOB resolution is not only caused by inactive bits as the name of the parameter ENOB suggests on the first glance, but also by noise that cannot be contributed to quantization noise.

Quantization noise is the result of quantizing the input signal and therefore is strongly correlated to the input signal (see fig 1).

It is obvious, that when a sine wave signal is used as stimulus data and the DAC under test generates the quantized output for several periods of the sine wave then the quantization error signal shows the same periodicity as the original sine wave. In the noise spectrum this means the quantization noise appears in bins at frequencies that are multiples of the sine wave frequency only. This specific property can additionally be used to separate any parasitic random noise in the test setup from the quantization noise relevant for the determination of the key performance parameters.

## Ensemble averaging

In the following, the proposed methodology for the improvement of the digitizer capability for measuring the quantization noise of the DAC is based on a noise model containing four noise sources. First, the DAC generates the quantization noise that needs to be determined as exact as possible at mission mode speed. Second, the DAC may also generate some random noise as a result of thermal noise and noise generated in the semiconductor junctions of the output stages. Third, a significant amount of random noise is generated in the ranging and offset correction amplifiers as well as in the input filters to the ATE digitizer. And fourth, the digitizer generates quantization noise due to its limited resolution. Both random noise sources are seen by the digitizer ADC and can be mapped into one common random noise source.

However, the noise power of the random noise source typically exceeds the quantization noise power of the DAC and prevents the ATE from measuring the SNR, SINAD and ENOB of the DAC under test correctly. A graphical representation of this noise model is shown in fig. 2.

The most intuitive method for the separation of the wanted quantization noise from any unwanted random noise is the use of averaging. Due to the coherency requirements typically N samples spread over M signal periods of a sine wave are captured for calculating the dynamic performance parameters of a DUT. However, when we call a sample set of N samples containing M signal periods an ensemble, we can also capture K ensembles and align them on top of each other to calculate an averaged ensemble (see fig. 3).

To achieve the desired result we simply average each of the N samples subsequently over the K ensembles. Since the random noise is not correlated to the signal and its periodicity, the averaging reduces the noise power by a factor of K when K ensembles had been captured for averaging. In contrast, since the quantization error has the same periodicity as the test signal and appears as discrete lines at multiples of the signal frequency, it will not be affected by the ensemble averaging. All other harmonic distortion components will not be affected either. Therefore ensemble averaging reduces the parasitic random noise whereas the deterministic noise will remain unchanged.

The improvement of ensemble averaging can easily be deduced from theory. Assumed the situation that only random noise would reduce the number of effective bits and the converter would have an infinite resolution we would get the following relationship:

ENOB(K)=ENOB(1)+10×log(K)/6.02

Formula (5)

In this relationship K denotes the number of ensembles used for averaging and ENOB(1) means no averaging applied. Vice versa we can rearrange this equation to determine how many ensembles are required for averaging to unveil a certain amount of effective quantization bits buried in a certain amount of random noise.

As soon as sufficient averaging is performed, the noise power can be calculated as usual by summing up the noise power in all bins except those of the fundamental. However care has to be taken for the first few harmonics. According to the SNR definition and in contrast to the definition of SINAD, a certain number of harmonics have to be excluded from the noise power sum (typically the 2nd to the 5th). As it can be seen from the previous discussion a certain amount of quantization noise power appears in these bins. But typically this error can be neglected due to the fact that for a large number of bits the quantization noise is wide spread over many bins.

Figure 4 shows the anticipated improvement in the deterministic ENOB calculation obtained from simulation. The DAC was assumed to be ideal with 12 bits (blue curve) and 16bits resolution (red curve) and the digitizer was assumed to be infinitesimally accurate. 1024 samples were used per ensemble and up to 128 ensembles were captured to perform ensemble averaging. The samples in an ensemble were spread over 7 periods of the sine wave signal. The ENOBs were calculated from the averaged ensemble by comparing the sum of the noise power in all spectrum bins to the fundamental. For both setups an additional random noise equivalent to 10 ENOBs was added.

In the simulation result it is visible that for both resolutions the determined ENOB result with no averaging is equivalent to the assumed random noise. This is because the random noise completely masks the quantization noise of both DAC resolutions. With the increasing number K of averages both curves initially follow the equation (5). However starting from a few averages on, the blue 12 bit curve starts to saturate and approaches the limit of 12 bits given by the DAC resolution. This means further averaging can no longer reduce the total noise which is now dominated by the 12bit quantization noise. The red curve however shows further improvement with the number of averages because the random noise is still much larger than the 16bit quantization noise even after averaging 128 ensembles. This means more averages would be required to unveil the quantization noise from the random noise.

In fig 5, the curves show the simulated ENOB result versus DAC resolution given in bits, based on a 12 bit digitizer in presence of random noise, equivalent to 10 ENOBs. The blue solid curve shows the result when no ensemble averaging is performed. This curve is clearly limited by the 10 bits of equivalent random noise. In contrast, the red solid line shows the result with averaging based on 64 ensembles. This clearly shows the limitation of the 12 bit digitizer as soon as the DAC resolution exceeds the digitizer resolution.

## Measurement results

In order to verify the described ensemble averaging methodology, a first experiment was made to quantify the amount of random noise seen by the unloaded digitizer and the ability to suppress it with ensemble averaging according to theory. For the experiment 64 ensembles containing 1024 samples each were captured in one shot. The 16bit digitizer of the V93000 MBAV8 instrument was running at maximum speed of 110Msps for this experiment. The 1V range and a 15MHz filter were used for the setup.

From fig. 6 it becomes obvious that inevitable random noise in the ranging and offset correction stages of the digitizer instrument impose a certain limitation to the measurement of the ENOB performance of about 10.5bit for a full scale sine, despite the fact that the idling input is terminated with 50ohms to ground.

However, when ensemble averaging is used, the measurable deterministic ENOBs increase significantly - in compliance with theory - to beyond 12.5 for 64 averaged ensembles. This indicates that using this methodology, a 12bit DAC can be tested correctly for its dynamic noise parameters despite the random noise in the setup.

In the next experiment a 16bit DAC was used as test object and a set of test pattern was generated to restrict the resolution to the upper N bits of the DAC (N=4-16 bits). The DAC was running at 200Msps, twice the speed of the MBAV8 digitizer and generated a 1MHz sine wave. Between the DAC under test and the digitizer we put a 10.7MHz low pass filter to separate an inband noise including the quantization noise of the DAC and an outband noise that should only contain noise from the ATE digitizer instrument. Again 64 ensembles with 1024 samples each were recorded. Post-processing was performed taking into account the different bandwidth to show the result without averaging and with averaging over 64 ensembles.

 ![Extending the Capabilities of ATE Digitizers for Dynamic Testing of High Speed, High Resolution DACs with Ensemble Averaging (9)](<../../../../raw/120453.png>)

Fig. 7: Separation of DAC and digitizer ENOBs using a 10.7MHz low pass filter

As fig. 7 shows, the outband noise is independent of the number of bits used for the DAC pattern, as expected. However, it can be seen clearly, that using an averaging of 64, the deterministic ENOBs calculated from the frequencies outside the passband improve from about 10.5 to beyond 13.

The deterministic ENOBs calculated from inside the 10.7MHz passband area show the expected increase with the number of DAC bits used as long as the random noise is smaller than the quantization noise, independent of averaging. However, without the ensemble averaging, the ENOBs determined are limited to the random noise equivalent to 10.5 ENOBs. In contrast, with ensemble averaging the saturation of the deterministic ENOBs occurs at about 13 bits which is the point where the quantization noise becomes smaller than the random noise when averaging 64 ensembles. According to equation (5), it would be required to at least average 32 ensembles for a correct measurement of a 13bit DAC quantization noise despite the 10.5 ENOBs of equivalent random noise. This is in good agreement with the measurement results.

## Conclusion

From the statistical analysis of the quantization error of a sinusoid the important result was obtained that the quantization noise and parasitic random noise in a test setup can be effectively distinguished by means of their randomness or determinism and their spectral properties. As a consequence post-processing methods can be tuned to the respective noise properties. After investigation of several options, ensemble averaging turned out to be an extremely effective and powerful means to suppress the parasitic random noise in the test setup and to significantly improve the accuracy of SNR, SINAD and deterministic ENOB calculations. The measurements performed show almost perfect agreement with theory and can therefore be considered as a proof of concept.
